## 1. Reading a Client: The Discovery Framework

Every consultation starts with understanding the client. You're not filling out a form — you're building a mental model of their business so you can map it to opportunities. Adapt the order, depth, and language based on who you're talking to.

### The Five Core Dimensions

1. **What does the company do?** — Industry, products/services, and critically, where those are made/delivered. Canadian value-add determines export eligibility. Manufacturing vs. service vs. tech vs. retail opens completely different program sets.

2. **Revenue and stage** — This gates everything. Pre-revenue companies have a fundamentally different grant profile than $500K companies, which have a different profile than $5M companies. Always establish this early — it determines which programs are even on the table and which service tier makes sense.

3. **Hiring plans** — Who are they hiring, when, what type of role? Student vs. full-time vs. contractor? Age of candidate, field of study, demographic factors? This opens the entire hiring grant category, which is the most universally accessible pillar.

4. **Training needs** — Are they sending existing employees to external courses? Planning certifications, technical training, leadership development? This opens training grants — often the easiest wins because the client was going to pay for training anyway.

5. **Growth and expansion** — Are they selling or planning to sell outside Canada? Which markets? Expanding domestically? Launching new product lines? Planning capital investments? This surfaces export grants, innovation programs, and sometimes sector-specific funding.

Always gather these five dimensions before making recommendations. If you have the client's profile from GetGranted 2.0, you may already have most of this — but verify, because situations change.

### Adapting Discovery by Client Type

The five dimensions stay the same, but HOW you probe them changes based on who's in front of you:

**Tech/Innovation Companies** — Probe for TRL (Technology Readiness Level), IP ownership, R&D activities, whether founders are on payroll (affects SR&ED eligibility), accelerator or incubator affiliations. Ask about existing grants (IRAP, NRC, university funding) to avoid overlap.

**Manufacturers** — Probe for Canadian value-add (what percentage of the product's value is created in Canada), in-house manufacturing vs. co-packing (co-packing means they lose manufacturer classification for grant purposes), equipment/automation plans, tariff exposure, export markets.

**CPG/Consumer Brands** — Probe for regulatory status (FDA, Health Canada), IP/formulation ownership, retail vs. DTC vs. wholesale mix, physical store locations (opens retail-specific grants), willingness to hire science students for R&D/testing.

**Service Businesses** — Probe for whether services can be exported (CanExport applies to services, not just products), multi-location operations (affects payroll structure and provincial program access), seasonal hiring patterns.

**Pre-Revenue Startups** — Probe for existing grant relationships (IRAP, university grants, accelerator funding), financial runway for cost-carry, incorporation status and date, co-founder structure, and whether they understand that most grants are reimbursement-based.

**Multi-Location Businesses** — Probe for payroll structure (single entity vs. multiple entities), provincial registrations, whether students can flex across locations, and whether each location qualifies independently.

**Family Businesses (Multi-Generational)** — Probe for decision-making structure: who's the champion vs. who controls the budget? In family businesses, the person on the call often isn't the final decision-maker. Map the roles (founder/parent = formulation/operations, next-gen = strategy/marketing, sibling = COO/logistics). Understand succession dynamics — a parent aging out changes hiring priorities and training needs. Front-load the value proposition for the budget holder if they have limited time on the call, then go deep with the champion after.

**Multi-Entity Owners** — Probe for how many businesses they own, the revenue and industry of each, and whether they share payroll/legal entities. Each business has a different grant profile — a tech startup and a 40-year family farm have completely different programs available. One subscription can often cover both, but map them separately. Ask: "Do you have other businesses? They might qualify for different programs."

**Recreational/Hospitality/Tourism Businesses** — Probe for: for-profit vs. not-for-profit status (gates which programs apply), multi-location payroll structure, seasonal staffing patterns (summer heavier = more students), whether students' academic programs align with the work (kinesiology for coaching = yes, music student for pickleball = no). These businesses often fall between standard grant categories — be honest about what fits and what doesn't.

**Forestry/Natural Resources Companies** — Probe for: whether they produce finished products vs. raw materials/intermediate goods (affects innovation program eligibility), whether they do value-add manufacturing in Canada, co-packing or outsourced production, First Nations project involvement, seasonal hiring patterns (spring/summer ramp-up), and whether they're in a northern/rural region (opens regional-specific programs). Forestry is receiving significant government funding focus — natural resources, greentech, and decarbonization programs are expanding.

**Raw Material Suppliers and Trading Companies** — Probe for Canadian value-add. Companies that buy, store, repack/rebrand, and re-export without substantial manufacturing are classified as middlemen — this disqualifies them from CanExport and most innovation programs. If they're acquiring manufacturing capability (e.g., purchasing a value-add facility), that changes the picture. Pin the conversation to "when that facility is operational, you'll qualify." Don't force programs around a classification that doesn't fit.

### What Discovery Reveals Beyond Program Fit

Good discovery also tells you:

- **Client sophistication level** — Have they applied before? Do they know specific programs? Are they starting from zero? This determines how much you explain vs. how quickly you move to strategy.
- **Decision-making structure** — Is this the decision-maker, or do they need to convince a partner, board, or family member? Adjust your pitch accordingly.
- **Bandwidth and capacity** — Can they manage applications themselves, or do they need full-service support? This directly affects service tier recommendations.
- **Urgency** — Is there a time-sensitive opportunity (program closing, hire starting, trip planned)? This reshapes your entire prioritization.

### Critical Eligibility Gates to Check Early

These are binary qualifiers that should be established in the first few minutes of discovery. If any of these fail, it reshapes the entire conversation:

- **One-year operations minimum** — Most grant programs require at least one year of business operations. This is based on the business registration date, not how long the person has been doing the work informally. If they're under one year, plant the seed and give them a specific date to come back.
- **Incorporation status** — Incorporated companies have more program access than sole proprietorships. Don't tell them to rush out and incorporate, but note the difference and recommend it as a future step. CanExport eligibility for sole proprietors is uncertain — escalate internally if it comes up.
- **Revenue thresholds** — CanExport requires $100K+ in revenue on the last T2/GST return. Many programs have implicit revenue floors. For high-growth companies, use projected revenue to assess fit but confirm cash flow can handle the reimbursement model.
- **Financial stability for cost-carry** — Almost all grants are reimbursement-based. The company must have cash to front costs. If they can't, grants may not be the right tool yet. Explain this early and compassionately.
- **Provincial registration** — Provincial programs require registration in that province. A federally incorporated company operating in BC needs extra-provincial registration to access BC programs. Always ask where they're registered, not just where they're located.

### Adapting Discovery by Contact Type

The five dimensions stay the same, but WHO you're talking to changes what you can accomplish on the call:

- **The champion (strategy/marketing lead)** — Usually the person who booked the call. Go deep on plans, growth, and opportunities. Give them the full roadmap. They'll sell it internally.
- **The budget holder (owner/CFO/parent)** — May have limited time. Front-load the ROI case and the value proposition. Use specific dollar ranges. Don't bury the lead in explanation.
- **The finance person** — Not marketing, not operations. Focus on the financial ROI, fee structures, and cashflow implications. Don't ask detailed marketing strategy questions they can't answer. Frame next steps as "discuss with your team."
- **The fractional/consultant (fractional COO, external advisor)** — Often sophisticated about business but may not know the company's specific plans. They're evaluating Granted as a service provider. Lead with credibility (success rate, process, differentiators) and propose a deeper call with the internal team.

## 2. Client Sizing: Matching Situation to Service

One of the most important consultant skills is rightsizing — recommending the service level that actually makes sense for the client's situation, even when that means recommending less. Overselling destroys trust and leads to poor outcomes. Underselling leaves money on the table.

### The Sizing Decision Tree

**Pre-revenue, no financial stability for co-funding:**
- NOT a fit for managed services at any tier
- Redirect to: SR&ED credits (if doing R&D), free database exploration, or paid consultation ($250) when they're more established
- Plant the seed: "Once you have revenue established, come back and we can build a full strategy"
- Don't make them feel dismissed — validate their ambition, be specific about what changes the equation

**Under one year of operations:**
- Most programs require one year minimum from business registration date
- Route to free tier (database access, no support) to keep them in the ecosystem
- Give them a specific date to re-engage: "Call me back in [month] when you've hit one year"
- If they're planning to hire before the one-year mark, they can start the hire now without a grant, then apply for the grant subsidy on the second term/renewal once eligible
- Referral potential: solo practitioners (bookkeepers, accountants) serving small businesses are natural referral partners — nurture them even when they're too small to be direct clients

**Revenue under ~$1-2M, limited grant needs:**
- Starter tier is the natural fit
- Includes strategy session to build a roadmap + portal access for ongoing matching
- Ad hoc advisory available for specific questions
- If they only have ONE small need (e.g., one student hire), even Starter may be too much — rightsize to a consultation instead
- Key test: will the annual fee + success fees leave enough of the grant value to make it worthwhile?

**Revenue $1-2M, high grant volume (10+ student hires or multi-entity):**
- Even below the typical Pro threshold, high volume of applications can justify Pro
- Key test: if Starter caps at 3 supported applications and they need 10+ student placements, the math favors Pro (lower success fee at 20% vs. 25%, unlimited applications)
- ProLite ($3,000/year + 20% success fee) exists as a middle tier for companies that need more than Starter but can't justify full Pro — positioned between Starter and Pro
- Multi-entity owners: one subscription can cover multiple businesses. Pricing for multi-company arrangements is handled case-by-case — check with the team for current packaging

**Revenue $2M+, multiple growth initiatives:**
- Pro tier makes sense when there are multiple avenues to pursue simultaneously
- Client needs a dedicated team managing applications, claims, and strategy across programs
- Full managed service: account manager, researchers, coordinators, writers
- Success-based fee structure aligns incentives

**Experienced applicants who know what they're doing:**
- Even at $3-5M revenue, if the client is competent and has applied successfully before, they may not need Pro
- Starter + one-off writing services (like CanExport writing) can be more cost-effective
- The test: "Would you do this yourself if you had the information?" If yes → platform + targeted support. If no → managed service.

**Niche businesses that don't fully align with Granted's core:**
- Be honest. If only 20% of what the client needs falls within Granted's wheelhouse, say so
- Capture what IS a fit (usually student hiring is universal), let go of what isn't
- Credibility comes from honesty, not from forcing a fit

### Sizing Principles

- **It's better to start small and upgrade than to oversell and disappoint.** A client who starts with Starter and upgrades to Pro in year two is a better outcome than one who buys Pro, gets minimal ROI, and leaves.
- **Upgrade credits preserve the relationship.** Consultation fees deduct from annual subscriptions. Starter fees pro-rate into Pro upgrades. Always mention this — it removes the risk of starting small.
- **Do the math out loud.** If a client would pay $1,700 + 25% of a $5K grant = $2,950 total cost on a $5K grant, acknowledge that the math is tight. Don't hide it.
- **Nine times out of ten, the answer to "should I get Pro?" is no.** Most businesses are better served by Starter + targeted support. Say this to build credibility.
- **10x ROI is the Pro qualification heuristic.** If you can't see a path to at least $50K in grant value against the $5K Pro investment, recommend Starter instead. This is Jorge's explicit threshold: "When I'm looking to work with a company, 10x is my minimum."
- **Proactively downgrade at renewal.** If a Pro client's circumstances change and they can't leverage 10x, move them to Starter at renewal rather than letting them overpay. Protecting the client's ROI protects the relationship.
- **The $5M threshold for Pro is situational, not absolute.** It depends on grant volume potential, not just revenue. A $1.7M company hiring 10 students justifies Pro. A $3M company with one small need doesn't. Let the volume of applications drive the tier recommendation.
- **"I don't send materials" — route to the product.** When prospects ask you to "just send a list of grants," decline. Grants change constantly. Route them to the Starter subscription as the always-current reference. Don't give away free deliverables that go stale.

## 3. Strategic Sequencing: How to Prioritize and Layer

When a client has multiple potential grant opportunities (most do), the consultant's job is to create a sequenced roadmap — not a laundry list. The order matters because bandwidth is limited, timing windows are real, and early wins build momentum.

### The Priority Framework

**1. Time-sensitive opportunities first.** Is a program about to close? Did something just open? Is the client about to make a spending decision that would disqualify them? Urgency overrides everything else.

**2. Highest dollar value.** Export grants at $25-50K typically dwarf individual hiring grants at $5-7K. If the client qualifies for both, the bigger opportunity deserves more attention — but don't ignore the smaller wins.

**3. Easiest wins.** Training grants for courses they're already taking. Hiring grants for candidates they've already identified. These require minimal effort and build confidence in the process, especially for first-time applicants.

**4. Stackable opportunities.** Can they get both a federal hiring grant AND a provincial one for the same employee? That doubles the value with marginal extra effort. Always check whether programs allow stacking.

**5. Pipeline building.** Even if a program isn't open right now, help the client prepare so they're ready when it opens. "This program opens in April — let's have your materials ready in March."

### Sequencing Patterns from Real Consultations

**Urgency-first (tariff-impacted manufacturer):**
Time-sensitive relief fund → parallel export application → ongoing hiring grants → training as steady-state

**Multi-avenue layering (growing consumer brand):**
Student hiring grants (immediate, easy) → export grant (bigger value, more planning) → training subsidies (ongoing) → R&D credits (longer-term)

**Downsell sequencing (pre-revenue startup):**
R&D tax credits (most relevant now) → free database access (self-serve) → paid consultation (when established)

**Single-need rightsizing (solo operator, one hire):**
One consultation → everything needed for one application → upgrade credit preserved for later

**Dual-stream parallel (company with both export and innovation needs):**
Two different streams of the same program running simultaneously for different markets/activities → hiring grants as parallel layer → timing pressure dictates order

**Specialist referral (outside core expertise):**
Identify the mismatch → pivot to adjacent opportunity within expertise → refer to specialist for the core need → preserve relationship for what IS a fit

**Capacity-constrained high-growth (cosmetics/CPG brand scaling fast):**
"Don't try to get everything now. You have big growth goals, you have limitations on capacity. Let's do this one, then this one, then this one." → CanExport for immediate market entry → digital adoption/technology assessment (when program reopens) → student hiring as capacity allows → training ongoing

**Forestry seasonal ramp-up (multiple forestry clients):**
Training grants for immediate certifications (first aid, silviculture) → student hiring programs for summer season (April openings) → Canada Summer Jobs for seasonal labor → post-graduate green jobs placements → CanExport only if international markets are in play

**Two-company owner (tech startup + established family business):**
Map each entity separately → established business: ETG training + student hiring (immediate ROI) → startup: tech student hiring + innovation programs (when past MVP) → CanExport for whichever entity has international plans → one subscription covers both

### The Golden Rule of Sequencing

**Don't try to do everything at once.** Even if five programs apply, the client has limited bandwidth. Pick the top two or three. Execute those well. Then layer on the next round. A scattered approach leads to weak applications and overwhelmed clients.

## 4. Communication Principles: How Grant Consultants Build Trust

The transcripts reveal consistent communication patterns that differentiate expert consultants from salespeople. These patterns apply whether you're an AI agent or a human advisor.

### Honesty as Strategy

- **Tell clients when something ISN'T a fit.** "I don't have much in terms of funding for expanding across Canada for your type of work." This builds more trust than stretching a program to sort-of-fit.
- **Acknowledge when you're not sure.** "I'd have to look at the specific rules — they just made changes two weeks ago." Never bluff.
- **Be transparent about the reimbursement model.** Clients often assume grants are free money upfront. Explain cost-carry clearly and early: you spend first, then get reimbursed with receipts. If they can't afford to front the costs, grants may not be the right tool yet.
- **Share competitive intelligence honestly.** "The government deprioritized this market — only 10% of the budget is going there this year." Clients respect honesty about headwinds.
- **Say no more than you say yes.** Selectivity is a quality signal. "We probably say no nine times out of ten when people want the pro service."

### Concrete Examples and Anchoring

- **Use specific dollar ranges.** "$5-7K per student per term" or "first-time applicants typically get $25-30K" — specificity builds confidence.
- **Reference success cases (anonymized).** "We worked with a company in a similar situation — they received $22K per hire through this program."
- **Create hypothetical scenarios.** "Say your project costs $40K — the grant covers 50%, so you'd get $20K back after you submit receipts."
- **Anchor to what they already know.** "It works like your accountant doing your taxes — you could do it yourself, but a professional catches things you'd miss."

### Reframing and Pivoting

- **When a program doesn't fit, immediately pivot to what does.** Never leave the client hanging with just a "no." The pivot should feel seamless: "That program won't work because of X, but here's what WILL work..."
- **When circumstances change, reframe the opportunity.** Market deprioritized? Pivot to a different market. Industry excluded? Pivot to an adjacent program. Co-packing? Reframe from manufacturer to consumer brand.
- **When downsizing a recommendation, frame it as protecting their ROI.** "I just want to make sure you get a good return on your investment" — not "you're too small for this."

### Planting Seeds for Future Engagement

- **Reference future opportunities.** "When you start manufacturing, there are manufacturing grants." "New programs are expected in April after the fiscal year resets."
- **Create upgrade paths.** "Start with this, and if you're seeing good traction, we can upgrade you to Pro and apply the credit."
- **Name specific things to watch for.** "If that student works out, we can reapply next term — just update the job description to show incremental responsibilities."
- **Be specific about what changes the equation.** "Once you hit $100K in revenue on your T2, you unlock export programs. When is your fiscal year-end?"

### Using Analogies

Good analogies make complex grant concepts immediately understandable:

- **Financial planner analogy:** "We're like a financial planner who says you should look at defense and banking funds — we tell you the categories that fit, without naming the specific ETFs. That's the deeper consultation."
- **Bakery line analogy:** "It's like lining up at a bakery. If you're first in line, you wait 10 minutes. Back of the line, you wait an hour. Apply early."
- **CRA analogy:** "It's like CRA — they just need to find the one thing they don't like and cut it. So always submit a budget with padding."
- **Accountant analogy:** "60% of Canadians don't do their own taxes. Free to do it yourself, but a professional catches gaps."
- **Grant team analogy:** "It's like having a grant team for $5,000/year. You can't hire anyone for that, even part-time."
- **Dating analogy (for piloting new approaches):** "Date before you get married — try one project first, see how it goes, then scale."
- **"Shaping the narrative" framing:** "Sometimes you can shape the focus of your initiative to make it fit the program. It's about having the experience and relationships with program managers to understand what they really, really want." — This is one of the clearest articulations of Granted's value beyond matching.
- **Training as retention tool:** "80% is substantial. It's a really small cost to upskill your team, and it can be seen as a benefit for workers to come back — you're investing in them." — Especially powerful for seasonal industries with high turnover.
- **Reframing retainers as projects:** "Grants can't pay for ongoing retainers. It needs to be a project with deliverables and an end date. Even if your vendor charges monthly, we'd need to scope it as 'they're doing this project for this lump sum.'" — Comes up frequently with web developers, marketing agencies, and consultants on retainer.
- **Four generations of entrepreneurship:** Steph shares her own family business background to build rapport with family business owners. Personal connection to the client's world builds trust faster than credentials.

### Urgency Without Pressure

- **Create awareness of timing without being pushy.** "I'm not trying to discourage you, but the sooner you apply, the faster they adjudicate."
- **Frame deadlines as logistics, not sales tactics.** "The program closes May 29th. Applications submitted in February get approved by June. Applications submitted in May don't get approved until November."
- **Acknowledge the client's reality.** "I know this feels like a lot — let's focus on the one that matters most right now."

### Managing Multi-Stakeholder Dynamics

- **Identify who makes the decision vs. who needs to be convinced.** In family businesses, the person on the call may not be the budget holder. Adjust your pitch to give them ammunition for the internal conversation.
- **Give the champion a clear ROI story to take back.** "Here's what I'd tell your partners: for $5K/year, we've historically seen clients access $10K+ minimum — that's a 2-5x return."
- **Address the skeptic's concern directly.** "Of course I'm going to say it's worth it — but honestly, I say no nine times out of ten. Here's why I think your situation is different..."

### Adapting Pitch by Prospect Sophistication

**Grant-naive (first-time, no experience):**
- Explain the reimbursement model early and clearly — this is the #1 misconception
- Walk through a concrete example with dollar amounts: "You pay $5,000 for training. The grant reimburses 80%, so you get $4,000 back."
- Explain that Granted doesn't provide grants — the government does. Granted helps you find and apply for them. This confusion is common.
- Use the "accountant doing your taxes" analogy to explain value-add

**Grant-aware (has heard of programs, maybe applied for CEBA or Canada Summer Jobs):**
- Skip the basics. Focus on what they're missing and how the landscape has changed.
- Lead with a specific opportunity they didn't know about: "There's a program that just opened that would fit your situation."
- Use the webinar-to-call pipeline: "What caught your eye from the webinar?" to find their interest area.

**Grant-sophisticated (knows SR&ED, IRAP, CanExport, has applied on their own):**
- Match their level. Don't explain what grants are — explain Granted's differentiation.
- Lead with insider intelligence they wouldn't have: "The government just changed the rules two weeks ago. The US is being deprioritized."
- Focus on bandwidth and expertise gaps, not awareness: "You know the big programs. The value is catching what you're missing and shaping the narrative."
- Share your success rate and selectivity as credibility signals.

**Skeptical/burned (applied before, got rejected, spent hundreds of hours):**
- Validate their frustration. Don't dismiss past rejections.
- Rebuild confidence with achievable wins: training grants and student hiring are fast, high-certainty, low-effort.
- Show how relationships with program managers change the equation: "We know the people who adjudicate these. We've asked for exceptions."
- Frame the cost of doing it wrong: "200 hours on an application that gets rejected — that's more expensive than our annual fee."

### Competitive Differentiation (How to Handle "How are you different from X?")

**vs. Fundica and match-only platforms:**
- "They match you to a grant name. We match you, interpret the 30-page guidelines into 2 pages, call the grantors to verify how many spots they have, notify you when criteria change, and help you apply. The database is step one — the intelligence layer is where the value lives."

**vs. SR&ED consultants:**
- "We don't do SR&ED or tax credits. We refer to specialist partners for that. We focus on hiring, training, and market expansion grants — the operational side. If you need both, we coordinate but stay in our lane."

**vs. Doing it yourself:**
- "You absolutely can. The portal gives you everything you need. But our managed clients have a 92% success rate — and that comes from 15 years of knowing exactly what grantors want to see. The question is whether your time is better spent running your business or learning the grant system."

**"Can a grant pay for Granted's fees?"**
- This comes up frequently. The answer is no — there's no current program that reimburses consulting fees for grant services. CDAP/SeedUp used to do this, but that program is gone. Acknowledge why they're asking (CDAP set the expectation), but be straightforward that it's not available.

## 5. Objection Handling: The Playbook

### "Is it worth the investment?"

- Do the math together. If the annual fee + success fees consume most of the grant value, it's NOT worth it — say so.
- If the math works, show it concretely: "$1,700 investment → average $10K in grants = 5x return."
- When the math is tight for managed service, rightsize to consultation or self-serve + one-off writing.
- Mention that consultation fees credit toward annual subscriptions if they upgrade.

### "There probably aren't many grants for us"

- Reframe: "I can't make that statement because grants change constantly. New programs launch every fiscal year."
- Give a concrete hidden opportunity example relevant to their situation.
- Anchor to the portal: "Companies who use our platform get 60% more grants than when they applied on their own."
- Position it as risk mitigation: "Even if you only find one program this year, the ROI is there."

### "We already know how to apply / We've done this before"

- Acknowledge their competence — don't patronize.
- Pivot to value beyond application help: "The rules change every year. What worked last year might not work this year."
- Share insider knowledge they wouldn't have: "The government just changed the rules two weeks ago" or "They've deprioritized this market."
- Position ongoing intelligence, not just application writing, as the value.

### "How much of our time will this take?"

- Be honest — it scales with complexity.
- Hiring grants: "Barely any input from you — we handle almost everything."
- Export grants: "Maybe 2-3 hours total over a couple weeks — we need your market knowledge."
- Complex innovation grants: "More involved — you'll need someone internally as our point person."
- Use bandwidth constraints as evidence for Pro service when appropriate.

### "We don't have bandwidth for this right now"

- Validate the concern.
- Use it as evidence for managed service: "That's exactly why we exist — we do it, you just provide the input."
- If they genuinely can't engage at all right now, don't force it. Plant the seed and give them a specific trigger to come back: "When you start hiring for that new location, call us."

### "We applied before and got rejected for a weird reason"

- Don't dismiss. Investigate.
- Leverage insider contacts and program relationships to understand what happened.
- Show how relationships with grantors help navigate bureaucratic gatekeeping.
- Use rejection as intelligence for a stronger re-application.

### "How do you differ from [competitor]?"

- Don't trash-talk competitors. Explain the substantive difference.
- Core differentiator: 30+ years of grant expertise vs. tech-only platforms that match but can't advise.
- "They can match you to a grant, but their purpose is to match you to a grant writer. We say do it yourself — and we give you the interpretation and best practices to do it well."
- "We're not just matching — we're interpreting. We call the grantors, we understand what they're actually looking for."
- Let the 92% success rate speak for itself.

### "I want guarantees"

- Be direct: "There are no guarantees in life."
- Provide context: the 92% success rate is from managed service. Portal self-serve depends on the user's effort.
- Reframe what the portal delivers: best practices routed from managed service experience, time savings of at least 2 hours per week in research, and notifications you'd never find on your own.

### "We already applied on our own and it worked / we know how to do this"

- Different from "we've done this before" — this person is actively succeeding without help.
- Don't try to sell what they don't need. Acknowledge their competence.
- Pivot to the FOMO angle: "The risk isn't the applications you're doing — it's the ones you don't know about."
- Reference specific program changes or new programs they may have missed.
- If they genuinely don't need managed service, Starter is the honest recommendation. Don't push Pro on a self-sufficient client.

### "Can you just tell me three grants I should apply for?"

- This is asking for Pro-level service on a free call. Hold firm but explain why.
- "The reason we don't do that is because for that level of service, you need a dedicated strategist who understands your full situation. That's what Pro is."
- Frame the matching platform as the answer for people who want to self-identify opportunities: "That's exactly what our tech does — you input your situation, it shows you what fits."
- Don't make them feel rejected for asking — redirect to the appropriate tier.

### "I'm worried we're too niche / we don't fit standard categories"

- Validate: some businesses genuinely fall between categories (recreational facilities, trading companies, creative businesses).
- Be honest about what fits and what doesn't: "I'd love to have you as a client. I just don't know that I can add the level of value you need in [the niche area]. But here's what IS a fit..."
- Capture what works (usually student hiring and training are universal), let go of what isn't.
- If they need municipal grants, indigenous community grants, sports grants, or other niche categories outside Granted's core — say so directly.

## 6. Red Flag Recognition: When to Say No

A critical consultant skill is knowing when something ISN'T a fit and being honest about it quickly. This saves everyone's time and builds long-term credibility.

### Hard Disqualifiers

- **Pre-revenue with no co-funding capacity** — Can't sustain the cost-carry model. Redirect to R&D credits and free resources.
- **Single small need where fees would consume the grant** — One $5K student grant minus $2,950 in fees = not worth it. Rightsize to consultation.
- **No tangible project or plan** — Grants fund specific activities. "We might do something eventually" isn't enough to apply.
- **Activities already completed** — Cannot claim retroactively for most programs. If they've already booked flights, signed contracts, or started work before applying, those expenses are likely ineligible.
- **Employee already on payroll** — Most hiring grants require applying BEFORE the hire. Can't retroactively fund someone who's already working.
- **Under one year of operations** — Most programs require minimum one year from business registration. Give them a specific re-engagement date rather than a vague "come back later."
- **Sole proprietorship seeking innovation/export grants** — Not a hard no, but significantly fewer programs are accessible. CanExport eligibility for sole proprietors is uncertain. Always check internally before committing.
- **Company moving to co-packers** — Loses manufacturer classification. This is a critical discovery signal: "Do you make it yourself, or do you use a co-packer?" The answer reshapes the entire program set. If they're transitioning manufacturing to co-packers, flag this proactively — they may lose eligibility for programs they previously qualified for.
- **Middleman/reseller without Canadian value-add** — Companies that buy, store, repack, and re-export without substantial manufacturing are excluded from CanExport and most innovation programs. A planned value-add facility changes the picture, but only once it's operational.

### Soft Red Flags (Investigate Further)

- **Niche business that doesn't fit standard categories** — Event companies, recreational facilities, creative businesses often fall between categories. Be honest about what's available vs. what isn't.
- **Monthly retainer-based vendor costs** — Grants require discrete projects with deliverables and end dates. Ongoing retainers don't qualify — the client may need to restructure their vendor relationship as a project.
- **Moving to co-packing** — Loses manufacturer classification. Changes the entire grant profile. Flag this proactively.
- **High sophistication, low need** — If the client already applies successfully on their own, they may not see enough value in paid services. Platform subscription may be the right level.
- **Multi-entity complexity** — Some clients own multiple businesses, some with partners, some solo. Each entity may have different eligibility. Don't assume — verify structure.
- **50%+ revenue from a single export market** — CanExport requires target markets to be <10% of revenue or <$100K. Companies heavily concentrated in one market (especially the US) may be immediately disqualified for their primary market. Redirect to exploring secondary markets they haven't prioritized.
- **In-house training only** — BC ETG requires third-party training providers. If the company does all training internally, the ETG play shifts to adjacent skills (first aid, digital skills, leadership, business development) rather than core trade skills. Don't let the client think their current training approach qualifies when it doesn't.
- **Remote-only company seeking student hires** — Not a disqualifier, but some programs emphasize the quality of the student experience, which can be harder to demonstrate in a fully remote setting. Probe for how they'd structure the placement.
- **Demographic funding misconceptions** — Clients often assume that being women-owned, youth-led, or BIPOC-owned means there's a dedicated bucket of grant money. The reality is that demographic factors typically increase subsidy rates within existing programs (70% instead of 50%, $7K instead of $5K) rather than opening entirely separate programs. Manage expectations early.

### How to Deliver a "No"

- **Never just say no.** Always pair it with what they CAN do, what would change the equation, or where else to look.
- **Be specific about why.** "Your revenue is below the threshold for this program" is better than "you don't qualify."
- **Preserve the relationship.** "Right now, this isn't a fit — but when X changes, come back and we'll build the strategy."
- **Rightsize rather than reject.** Even if Pro isn't right, maybe Starter works. Even if Starter isn't right, maybe a consultation covers it.

## 7. Timing as a Strategic Lever

Timing is one of the most powerful tools in grant strategy. The same company with the same needs can get dramatically different results depending on WHEN they apply, WHEN they start spending, and WHEN they file their tax returns.

### Universal Timing Principles

**Apply before you spend.** This is the single most important timing rule across almost all programs. Once you've committed money (booked flights, signed contracts, put down deposits), those expenses often become ineligible. The sequence is: apply → get approved → then execute and spend. There are rare exceptions (some retroactive programs), but the default assumption should always be: don't spend before the grant is in place.

**Earlier applications get faster decisions.** Programs adjudicate roughly in order of receipt. February applications may be approved by April. May applications might not be approved until November. Same program, same quality — just different timing.

**Government fiscal year drives everything.** The Canadian government fiscal year starts April 1. New programs launch, existing programs get new funding tranches, and program rules may change. The weeks before and after April 1 are critical planning periods.

**Programs change mid-cycle.** Don't assume rules from last year apply this year. Rules can change AFTER a program opens. A key value of working with consultants or a monitoring platform is staying current on these changes.

**Build readiness before windows open.** When a program is closed but expected to reopen, use the downtime to prepare. Have documents ready, job descriptions written, market research done. When the window opens, be first in line.

### Timing Tactics

- **Fiscal year-end strategy:** If a startup is approaching a revenue threshold (e.g., $100K for export program eligibility), a strategic short fiscal year-end can realize that revenue on their T2 sooner, unlocking programs months earlier.
- **Semester alignment:** Student hiring programs run on academic semesters. If hiring in July, it may be better to start the grant placement in September for a full term of coverage rather than catching the tail end of the summer term.
- **Re-application timing:** If a student works out, you can reapply for subsequent terms — just update the job description to show incremental responsibilities.
- **Early deposit warnings:** At trade shows and events, organizers often offer "early bird" discounts on booth deposits. But if those deposits are made before grant approval, the expense may be ineligible. Warn clients to hold off on committing funds until timing is clear.
- **"Don't commit before approval" principle:** At trade shows and events, organizers dangle early-bird discounts on next year's booth. But if the deposit is made before grant approval, and the program requires you to be "reliant on the grant," that expense becomes ineligible. Some programs (like CanExport) can have pre-approval expenses written in, but the safest default is: don't spend until you've applied. If you MUST book before approval and the grant comes through, expenses may still be reimbursed — but if the grant is rejected, you're out of pocket.
- **End-of-fiscal awareness:** Late February through March is the thinnest period for program availability. Most programs have run out of funds or are winding down. After April 1 (new federal fiscal year), expect a wave of new programs and renewed funding. Use this context to explain to clients why pickings are slim right now and set expectations for what opens next month.
- **Training grant fiscal year boundary:** Training happening before April must be applied for under the current fiscal year — apply immediately. Training after April waits for the new fiscal year to open. Missing this boundary means missing funding entirely.
- **Student hiring timing warning:** Students already on payroll CAN still be grant-eligible, but you need to justify why you're placing them under a student hiring program retroactively. Better to align start dates with grant program cycles (April for summer term). "It's okay they're already working for you, but you have to justify them being under a student program when you started them two months before."
- **Incremental responsibilities for re-application:** When re-applying for the same student in a subsequent term, update the job description to show increased responsibilities. This is the documented requirement for re-application and can stack a single student to $14K+ across multiple terms.

## 8. The Grant Strategy Cycle: Ongoing Relationship Management

Grant strategy is not a one-time event. It's an annual cycle of planning, applying, monitoring, and adjusting.

### The Annual Rhythm

1. **Annual strategy meeting** — Deep review of business goals, identification of all applicable programs, creation of a prioritized grant roadmap for the year. In managed service, this is quarterly.

2. **Ongoing monitoring** — Platform sends matched grant notifications weekly. New programs launch, existing programs change criteria, funding tranches open and close. Clients need to stay engaged, not just set and forget.

3. **Trigger-based action** — Whenever a client is hiring, starting training, planning expansion, or making a significant business change, check for applicable grants. The best consultants train their clients to notify them about changes BEFORE they happen.

4. **Application and claims management** — For managed clients, the consultant handles application prep, submission, follow-up, and claims processing. For self-serve clients, the platform and advisory services support them through the process.

### What Clients Need to Understand

- **Forward government communications immediately.** When the government contacts them about an application, they MUST forward those emails to their consultant within 2 business days. Delayed responses can result in grants being pulled.
- **Apply before starting.** Especially for training grants — the application must be submitted before training begins. Even one day late is too late.
- **Plans change, and that's okay.** If a hire quits, a course gets cancelled, or expansion plans shift — notify the consultant immediately. Early notification allows for adjustments. Waiting until the end of the funding period creates problems.
- **Grants are a long game.** A company that stays engaged with grant strategy year over year will capture significantly more funding than one that applies once and walks away.

### Service Tier Progression

The tiers aren't just pricing — they're a client lifecycle:

**Free tier** → Database access, no support. For prospects not ready for any paid tier (pre-revenue, under one year, too small). Keeps them in the ecosystem. Good for referral partners (bookkeepers, accountants) who serve small businesses.

**Starter ($1,700/year + 25% success fee)** → Self-serve with up to 3 supported applications. For companies under ~$2M with limited grant needs, or experienced applicants who want the intelligence layer but can execute themselves.

**ProLite ($3,000/year + 20% success fee)** → Middle tier for companies that need more than 3 applications but can't justify full Pro. Positioned for mid-range clients after 6 months on Starter.

**Pro ($5,000/year + 20% success fee)** → Full managed service. Dedicated account manager, research team, coordinators, writers. For companies with 10x ROI potential ($50K+ in grant value). Quarterly strategy meetings minimum, weekly email notifications, proactive program monitoring.

**Upgrade path:** Consultation fees credit toward annual subscriptions. Starter fees pro-rate into Pro or ProLite upgrades. Always mention this — it removes the risk of starting small. Coupon codes ($200 off) are available as a closing tool for on-the-fence Starter prospects.

**Downgrade at renewal:** If a Pro client's circumstances change (fewer hires, paused expansion), proactively move them to Starter rather than letting them overpay. This protects the relationship and the 92% success rate.

## 9. Strategic Boundaries: What the Agent Should NOT Do

- **Don't make promises about approval.** You can assess readiness and strength, but the government makes the final decision. Use language like "strong candidate" or "based on what I've seen" — not "you'll definitely get this."
- **Don't recommend applying for programs that are a poor fit.** It's better to be honest and save the client time than to let them invest effort in a weak application.
- **Don't give tax or legal advice.** You can explain program rules and flag things like SR&ED implications or IP protection needs, but recommend professionals for actual tax, legal, or compliance decisions.
- **Don't speculate about program changes you don't know about.** If you don't know whether a program is still accepting applications, say so and suggest verifying with the program or the client's Grant Coordinator.
- **Don't try to replace the human consultant for complex strategic decisions.** Multi-program portfolio planning, nuanced eligibility edge cases, industry-specific expertise (like agri-food or clean tech), and high-stakes application strategy benefit from human judgment. Recommend the client speak with their Grant Coordinator at Granted.
- **Don't share specific program names in lead-generation contexts.** In early-stage conversations (before the client is a subscriber or Pro client), provide strategic category-level intelligence — "there are hiring subsidies in the $5-7K range per student" — without naming specific programs. This demonstrates expertise while protecting proprietary knowledge.
- **Don't position grants as free money.** Grants are reimbursement-based, require real spending, involve paperwork, and have compliance requirements. Frame them accurately: a powerful tool to stretch dollars and offset costs, not a windfall.

## 10. Tactical Reference: Program Mechanics and Operational Detail

This section captures operational details that consultants need to know but that don't fit neatly into the strategic framework above. It's the tactical companion to the strategic sections.

### The Three Pillars — Operational Detail

**Hiring Grants — Two Streams:**

*Student/Intern Stream:*
- $5-7K per student per term (4 months), depending on student characteristics
- Minority students, students with disabilities, and students from equity-deserving groups tend to qualify for the higher $7K tier
- Must be enrolled at a recognized post-secondary institution (bachelor's, master's, PhD)
- High school students do NOT qualify for standard student programs — redirect to Canada Summer Jobs (ages 15-30, up to 50% of minimum wage for 16 weeks, $8-11K per hire)
- Most programs require a co-op letter from the university — not all, but most
- Can reapply each term for the same student with documented incremental responsibilities
- Student's academic program should align with the work — kinesiology for coaching works, music student for pickleball doesn't
- Multi-location businesses with single payroll entity can flex students across locations
- 80-90% of hiring grants are NOT retroactive — must apply before the start date
- Granted acts as a third-party buffer for eligibility vetting: programs require candidate characteristics (age, status, disability) that employers can't legally ask about in hiring

*Post-Graduate/Youth Stream:*
- $15-30K per hire for niche, highly technical roles
- Under 30, graduated, working in specialized fields (tech, greentech, decarbonization, forestry, accounting)
- Not as widely available as student programs — tend to open/close with fiscal year cycles
- Newcomers to Canada (within last 10 years) working in cleantech: $22K per hire with 2-week turnaround
- Green jobs programs: $15-20K per hire, popular with forestry clients
- Senior/VP-level hiring programs exist but are more prevalent in Alberta than BC
- Fast turnaround times compared to larger grants
- Non-repayable, reimbursement-based

**Training Grants — BC ETG Detail:**
- Up to 80% of total training cost per employee
- Training must be provided by a third party within the province — in-house training is NOT eligible
- Must relate to the employee's job
- Must NOT be a degree program (bachelor's, master's) — short courses, certifications, and skills training only
- Business owners ARE eligible in BC (not in Alberta or Ontario)
- Turnaround: 3-4 weeks for approval
- Application effort: a couple of hours to half a day depending on complexity
- Must apply BEFORE training starts — even one day late is too late
- Fiscal year runs April-March — training before April must be applied for under current fiscal, training after April waits for new fiscal
- Organization went on strike for 3 months in 2025 — impacted client applications but resumed successfully
- Eligible examples: first aid, fire suppression, leadership, lean management, PMP, sales training, SEO, AI courses, wine education, ERP system training, silviculture, tree assessment
- NOT eligible: in-house training, degree programs, off-the-shelf software like Microsoft Office
- For ERP implementations: grant covers training costs ONLY, not implementation/software costs. Coach clients to ask vendors to break out training as a separate line item with modules, outcomes, time, and cost
- Approved provider list exists and can be shared with clients as a value-add

**CanExport — Key Operational Detail:**
- $31M total program budget (2026)
- US heavily deprioritized: only 10% of budget allocated to US projects, with defense/cleantech prioritized within that 10%
- Non-US, non-defense applications are playing "against the odds" for the US allocation
- First-time applicants: typically approved for $25-30K, not the full $50K
- Program allows up to 5 target markets per application, but recommend maximum 3 for a strong application
- Apply early: February applications approved by April/May. May applications not approved until November. Same program, same quality — just timing.
- Last day to apply: May 29 (2026 cycle). Activities must occur April 1 – March 31 of the funding year.
- Services ARE eligible (not just products): B2B software, consulting services, franchise operations all qualify
- Middleman/reseller exclusion: companies that buy, repack, and re-export without Canadian value-add manufacturing are excluded
- Digital marketing/ad spend removed from eligible expenses as of 2025
- Retainer-based vendor costs must be restructured as project-based with deliverables
- Pop-up events must be connected to a trade show being attended — can't be standalone
- Writing fee: $3,000 upfront for Pro clients (deposit against 20% success fee), $6,000 flat fee for Starter clients (not success-based)
- One project per year, but can target multiple countries in a single project
- Yearly reapplication possible: once revenue in a target market exceeds $100K or 10%, that market becomes ineligible, but other markets can still be targeted

### What Grants Generally Do NOT Cover
- Operating costs (rent, utilities, services)
- General capital/expansion costs (expanding a shop, buying equipment, real estate)
- Wages and salaries (except through specific hiring subsidy programs)
- Degree programs (MBA, bachelor's, master's)
- In-house training
- Off-the-shelf software
- **NEW EXCEPTION — Capital cost grants are emerging:** A new wave of programs now covers capital costs for specific purposes: accessibility improvements (up to $1M at 100%), forest industry transformation (IFIT, up to $5M at 50%), tariff relief (RTRI, up to $1M). These are purpose-specific and competitive, but they represent a shift from the traditional "grants don't cover capital" rule.

### R&D and Adjacent Programs (Awareness Only)
Granted does NOT manage these directly but the agent should know enough to flag and refer:

- **SR&ED** — Tax credit reimburses ~64% of eligible R&D salaries. Founders/developers MUST be on payroll (not sweat equity) to claim. Refer to specialist partners. Granted doesn't handle SR&ED or tax credits.
- **IRAP** — NRC program for R&D funding. Requires ~$250K+ revenue and 3-5 FTEs. Clients should maintain relationship with their ITA via quarterly email updates.
- **Mitacs** — Funds research collaborations with universities. Good for PhD/master's student placements.
- **Innovation grants (TRL framework)** — Most innovation grants target Technology Readiness Level 4-6+ (beyond concept, approaching commercialization). Pre-MVP companies are generally better served by SR&ED credits. When a client asks about innovation funding, assess their TRL first.

### Candidate Pool Prioritization
When a client is hiring, check whether candidates fall into priority categories (higher reimbursement rates or more program options):
1. STEM / digital technology — highest funding availability
2. Clean/green positions — significant government focus
3. Youth overcoming barriers (disability, mental health) — dedicated streams, often higher rates
4. Visible minorities, Indigenous, women in underrepresented fields — higher rates within programs
5. Newcomers/immigrants — dedicated programs regardless of age
6. General post-secondary graduates — good but more competitive
7. High school graduates / no post-secondary — most limited options

### Company Size Heuristics
- **Under $500K, <5 employees:** Student hiring and training grants only. CanExport not available. Free tier or Starter at most. May not hit one-year operations minimum yet.
- **$500K–$2M, 5-15 employees:** Full hiring and training access. CanExport becomes available at $100K revenue. 1-3 applications per year. Starter is the natural fit.
- **$2M–$5M, 15-50 employees:** Full range available. Multiple hires, training across teams, CanExport for international growth. Pro makes sense if volume justifies 10x ROI.
- **$5M+, 50+ employees:** Large-scale training budgets, multiple simultaneous hiring grants, complex CanExport projects. Pro is the natural fit. May also qualify for IRAP, SRED, and capital cost programs.

### Service Boundaries Reminder
- Granted does NOT work with: not-for-profits, SR&ED/tax credits, contests, loans, municipal grants, sports/community grants, indigenous community grants (though can advise on vendor partnerships)
- Granted DOES work with: hiring, training, market expansion, and the emerging capital cost programs (RTRI, IFIT, accessibility) for for-profit businesses
- For services outside Granted's scope, refer to specialist partners and be transparent about the boundary
