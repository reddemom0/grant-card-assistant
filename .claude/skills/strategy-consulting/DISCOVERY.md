# Discovery: Reading a Client

How Granted's consultants build a mental model of a business before recommending anything. Use this to prep a consultant for a discovery or strategy call, to review an intake for gaps, or to generate the right probes for a specific client type.

Every consultation starts with understanding the client. Discovery is not form-filling — it's building a mental model of the business to map it to opportunities. Help the consultant adapt the order, depth, and language to who they're talking to.

## The Five Core Dimensions

1. **What does the company do?** — Industry, products/services, and critically, where those are made/delivered. Canadian value-add determines export eligibility. Manufacturing vs. service vs. tech vs. retail opens completely different program sets.

2. **Revenue and stage** — This gates everything. Pre-revenue companies have a fundamentally different grant profile than $500K companies, which differ again from $5M companies. Establish this early — it determines which programs are even on the table and which service tier makes sense.

3. **Hiring plans** — Who are they hiring, when, what type of role? Student vs. full-time vs. contractor? Age of candidate, field of study, demographic factors? This opens the entire hiring grant category — the most universally accessible pillar.

4. **Training needs** — Are they sending existing employees to external courses? Planning certifications, technical training, leadership development? Training grants are often the easiest wins because the client was going to pay for the training anyway.

5. **Growth and expansion** — Selling or planning to sell outside Canada? Which markets? Expanding domestically? New product lines? Capital investments? This surfaces export grants, innovation programs, and sector-specific funding.

All five dimensions should be covered before recommendations are made. If the client's profile already exists (HubSpot record, prior deals, GetGranted profile), pull it first — but remind the consultant to verify, because situations change.

## Adapting Discovery by Client Type

The five dimensions stay the same; HOW to probe changes with who's in front of the consultant:

**Tech/Innovation Companies** — Probe for TRL (Technology Readiness Level), IP ownership, R&D activities, whether founders are on payroll (affects SR&ED eligibility), accelerator or incubator affiliations. Ask about existing grants (IRAP, NRC, university funding) to avoid overlap.

**Manufacturers** — Probe for Canadian value-add (what percentage of the product's value is created in Canada), in-house manufacturing vs. co-packing (co-packing loses manufacturer classification for grant purposes), equipment/automation plans, tariff exposure, export markets.

**CPG/Consumer Brands** — Probe for regulatory status (FDA, Health Canada), IP/formulation ownership, retail vs. DTC vs. wholesale mix, physical store locations (opens retail-specific grants), willingness to hire science students for R&D/testing.

**Service Businesses** — Probe for whether services can be exported (CanExport applies to services, not just products), multi-location operations (affects payroll structure and provincial program access), seasonal hiring patterns.

**Pre-Revenue Startups** — Probe for existing grant relationships (IRAP, university grants, accelerator funding), financial runway for cost-carry, incorporation status and date, co-founder structure, and whether they understand that most grants are reimbursement-based.

**Multi-Location Businesses** — Probe for payroll structure (single entity vs. multiple entities), provincial registrations, whether students can flex across locations, and whether each location qualifies independently.

**Family Businesses (Multi-Generational)** — Probe for decision-making structure: who's the champion vs. who controls the budget? The person on the call often isn't the final decision-maker. Map the roles (founder/parent = formulation/operations, next-gen = strategy/marketing, sibling = COO/logistics). Understand succession dynamics — a parent aging out changes hiring priorities and training needs. Coach the consultant to front-load the value proposition for the budget holder if they have limited time on the call, then go deep with the champion after.

**Multi-Entity Owners** — Probe for how many businesses they own, the revenue and industry of each, and whether they share payroll/legal entities. Each business has a different grant profile — a tech startup and a 40-year family farm have completely different programs available. One subscription can often cover both, but map them separately. Suggest asking: "Do you have other businesses? They might qualify for different programs."

**Recreational/Hospitality/Tourism Businesses** — Probe for: for-profit vs. not-for-profit status (gates which programs apply), multi-location payroll structure, seasonal staffing patterns (summer heavier = more students), whether students' academic programs align with the work (kinesiology for coaching = yes, music student for pickleball = no). These businesses often fall between standard grant categories — the honest read matters.

**Forestry/Natural Resources Companies** — Probe for: finished products vs. raw materials/intermediate goods (affects innovation program eligibility), value-add manufacturing in Canada, co-packing or outsourced production, First Nations project involvement, seasonal hiring patterns (spring/summer ramp-up), and northern/rural location (opens regional-specific programs). Forestry is receiving significant government funding focus — natural resources, greentech, and decarbonization programs are expanding.

**Raw Material Suppliers and Trading Companies** — Probe for Canadian value-add. Companies that buy, store, repack/rebrand, and re-export without substantial manufacturing are classified as middlemen — this disqualifies them from CanExport and most innovation programs. If they're acquiring manufacturing capability (e.g., purchasing a value-add facility), that changes the picture: pin the conversation to "when that facility is operational, you'll qualify." Don't help force programs around a classification that doesn't fit.

## What Discovery Reveals Beyond Program Fit

Good discovery also tells the consultant:

- **Client sophistication level** — Applied before? Know specific programs? Starting from zero? Determines how much explaining vs. how quickly to move to strategy.
- **Decision-making structure** — Is this the decision-maker, or do they need to convince a partner, board, or family member? The pitch adjusts accordingly.
- **Bandwidth and capacity** — Can they manage applications themselves, or do they need full-service support? Feeds directly into the tier recommendation (see `SIZING`).
- **Urgency** — Time-sensitive opportunity (program closing, hire starting, trip planned)? Reshapes the entire prioritization (see `SEQUENCING` and `TIMING`).

## Critical Eligibility Gates to Check Early

Binary qualifiers to establish in the first few minutes. If any fail, it reshapes the whole conversation:

- **One-year operations minimum** — Most programs require at least one year of business operations, based on the business registration date, not how long the person has worked informally. If under one year, plant the seed and give a specific date to come back.
- **Incorporation status** — Incorporated companies have more program access than sole proprietorships. Don't push them to rush incorporation, but note the difference and flag it as a future step. CanExport eligibility for sole proprietors is uncertain — the consultant should escalate internally if it comes up.
- **Revenue thresholds** — CanExport requires $100K+ in revenue on the last T2/GST return. Many programs have implicit revenue floors. For high-growth companies, use projected revenue to assess fit but confirm cash flow can handle the reimbursement model.
- **Financial stability for cost-carry** — Almost all grants are reimbursement-based: the company must have cash to front costs. If they can't, grants may not be the right tool yet. This should be raised early and compassionately.
- **Provincial registration** — Provincial programs require registration in that province. A federally incorporated company operating in BC needs extra-provincial registration to access BC programs. Always ask where they're registered, not just where they're located.

## Adapting Discovery by Contact Type

WHO the consultant is talking to changes what the call can accomplish:

- **The champion (strategy/marketing lead)** — Usually the person who booked the call. Go deep on plans, growth, and opportunities. Give them the full roadmap — they'll sell it internally.
- **The budget holder (owner/CFO/parent)** — May have limited time. Front-load the ROI case and the value proposition with specific dollar ranges. Don't bury the lead in explanation.
- **The finance person** — Not marketing, not operations. Focus on financial ROI, fee structures, and cashflow implications. Don't ask detailed marketing strategy questions they can't answer. Frame next steps as "discuss with your team."
- **The fractional/consultant (fractional COO, external advisor)** — Often business-sophisticated but may not know the company's specific plans. They're evaluating Granted as a service provider. Lead with credibility (success rate, process, differentiators) and propose a deeper call with the internal team.

## How to Use This for Call Prep

When a consultant asks for prep on a specific client:
1. Pull what's already known (HubSpot company/contact/deals, prior meeting notes via Granola if available)
2. Identify which of the five dimensions are already answered vs. open
3. Match the client to the closest client-type profile(s) above and pull those probes
4. Check the five eligibility gates against known facts; flag any at risk
5. Note the likely contact type and how the consultant should shape the call
6. Output: a one-page prep sheet — known facts, open questions ordered by importance, gates to confirm, and any red flags to watch (see `RED_FLAGS`)
