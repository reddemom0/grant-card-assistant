# Client Conversations: Prep Material

How Granted's best consultants build trust, frame value, and handle pushback. This is PREP material for the consultant — patterns and plays to have ready before a call, not scripts to read verbatim. When a consultant asks "how do I handle X," give them the relevant play plus the reasoning behind it so they can make it their own.

## Trust-Building Principles

**Honesty as strategy:**
- Tell clients when something ISN'T a fit ("I don't have much funding for expanding across Canada for your type of work"). This builds more trust than stretching a program to sort-of-fit.
- Acknowledge uncertainty ("I'd have to look at the specific rules — they changed two weeks ago"). Never bluff.
- Be transparent about the reimbursement model, clearly and early: you spend first, then get reimbursed with receipts. If they can't front the costs, grants may not be the right tool yet. This is the #1 client misconception.
- Share competitive intelligence honestly ("The government deprioritized this market — only 10% of the budget is going there this year"). Clients respect honesty about headwinds.
- Say no more than yes. Selectivity is a quality signal: "We probably say no nine times out of ten when people want the Pro service."

**Concrete examples and anchoring:**
- Specific dollar ranges build confidence: "$5-7K per student per term," "first-time applicants typically get $25-30K."
- Anonymized success cases: "We worked with a company in a similar situation — they received $22K per hire through this program." (Pull real ones from `search_recent_wins` and `get_program_stats` rather than relying on memory.)
- Hypothetical scenarios: "Say your project costs $40K — the grant covers 50%, so you'd get $20K back after you submit receipts."
- Anchor to what they already know: "It works like your accountant doing your taxes — you could do it yourself, but a professional catches things you'd miss."

**Reframing and pivoting:**
- When a program doesn't fit, pivot immediately to what does. Never leave the client with a bare "no": "That won't work because of X, but here's what WILL work..."
- When circumstances change, reframe the opportunity: market deprioritized → different market; industry excluded → adjacent program; co-packing → reframe from manufacturer to consumer brand.
- When downsizing a recommendation, frame it as protecting their ROI: "I want to make sure you get a good return on your investment" — not "you're too small for this."

**Planting seeds for future engagement:**
- Reference future opportunities: "When you start manufacturing, there are manufacturing grants." "New programs are expected in April after the fiscal reset."
- Create upgrade paths: "Start with this, and if you're seeing traction, we can upgrade you and apply the credit."
- Name specific things to watch for: "If that student works out, we can reapply next term — just update the job description to show incremental responsibilities."
- Be specific about what changes the equation: "Once you hit $100K in revenue on your T2, you unlock export programs. When is your fiscal year-end?"

**Urgency without pressure:**
- Timing awareness, not push: "I'm not trying to discourage you, but the sooner you apply, the faster they adjudicate."
- Deadlines as logistics, not sales tactics: "The program closes May 29th. February applications get approved by June; May applications wait until November."
- Acknowledge their reality: "I know this feels like a lot — let's focus on the one that matters most right now."

## Analogies That Land

Have these ready — they make grant concepts immediately understandable:

- **Financial planner:** "We're like a financial planner who says you should look at defense and banking funds — we tell you the categories that fit. The deeper consultation is where specifics come in."
- **Bakery line:** "If you're first in line, you wait 10 minutes. Back of the line, an hour. Apply early."
- **CRA:** "They just need to find the one thing they don't like and cut it. Always submit a budget with padding."
- **Accountant:** "60% of Canadians don't do their own taxes. Free to do it yourself, but a professional catches gaps."
- **Grant team:** "It's like having a whole grant team for less than a fraction of one part-time hire."
- **Dating (for piloting):** "Date before you get married — try one project first, see how it goes, then scale."
- **Shaping the narrative:** "Sometimes you can shape the focus of your initiative to fit the program. It's about the experience and relationships with program managers to know what they really want." — one of the clearest articulations of Granted's value beyond matching.
- **Training as retention:** "80% coverage is substantial. Small cost to upskill your team, and workers see it as investment in them — they come back." Especially powerful in seasonal industries with turnover.
- **Retainers as projects:** "Grants can't pay for ongoing retainers. It needs to be a project with deliverables and an end date. Even if your vendor bills monthly, we'd scope it as 'this project for this lump sum.'" — comes up constantly with web developers, marketing agencies, consultants on retainer.
- **Personal connection:** Sharing your own relevant background (e.g., a family-business upbringing with family-business owners) builds trust faster than credentials.

## Managing Multi-Stakeholder Dynamics

- Identify who makes the decision vs. who needs convincing. In family businesses, the person on the call may not hold the budget. The consultant's job is to arm them for the internal conversation.
- Give the champion a clear ROI story to carry: frame the annual fee against historically observed grant value — a concrete multiple, not vague promise.
- Address the skeptic head-on: "Of course I'm going to say it's worth it — but honestly, I say no nine times out of ten. Here's why your situation is different..."

## Adapting to Prospect Sophistication

**Grant-naive (first-time, no experience):**
- Explain the reimbursement model early — the #1 misconception
- Walk a concrete example with dollar amounts: "You pay $5,000 for training. The grant reimburses 80%, so $4,000 back."
- Clarify that Granted doesn't provide grants — the government does; Granted finds and wins them. This confusion is common.
- Use the accountant analogy for the value-add

**Grant-aware (has heard of programs, maybe applied for CEBA or Canada Summer Jobs):**
- Skip the basics. Focus on what they're missing and how the landscape changed.
- Lead with a specific opportunity they didn't know about.
- Webinar-to-call pipeline: "What caught your eye from the webinar?" locates their interest area.

**Grant-sophisticated (knows SR&ED, IRAP, CanExport; has applied solo):**
- Match their level; don't explain what grants are — explain Granted's differentiation.
- Lead with insider intelligence they wouldn't have: rule changes, budget reallocations, deprioritized markets.
- Focus on bandwidth and expertise gaps, not awareness: "You know the big programs. The value is catching what you're missing and shaping the narrative."
- Success rate and selectivity as credibility signals.

**Skeptical/burned (applied before, rejected, sunk hundreds of hours):**
- Validate the frustration; don't dismiss past rejections.
- Rebuild confidence with achievable wins: training grants and student hiring are fast, high-certainty, low-effort.
- Relationships change the equation: "We know the people who adjudicate these. We've asked for exceptions."
- Frame the cost of doing it wrong: "200 hours on a rejected application costs more than our annual fee."

## Competitive Differentiation

For "how are you different from X?" — the substantive answers, never trash talk:

**vs. Fundica and match-only platforms:**
"They match you to a grant name. We match you, interpret the 30-page guidelines into 2 pages, call the grantors to verify how many spots remain, notify you when criteria change, and help you apply. The database is step one — the intelligence layer is where the value lives."

**vs. SR&ED consultants:**
"We don't do SR&ED or tax credits — we refer to specialist partners for that. We focus on hiring, training, and market expansion grants — the operational side. If you need both, we coordinate but stay in our lane."

**vs. doing it yourself:**
"You absolutely can. The portal gives you everything you need. But our managed clients have a 92% success rate — that comes from 15 years of knowing exactly what grantors want to see. The question is whether your time is better spent running your business or learning the grant system." (Verify the current success-rate stat with `get_program_stats` before the consultant quotes it.)

**"Can a grant pay for Granted's fees?"**
Comes up frequently. The answer is no — no current program reimburses consulting fees for grant services. CDAP/SeedUp used to, and set that expectation; acknowledge why they're asking, be straightforward that it's gone.

## The Objection Playbook

Eleven recurring objections and how experienced consultants handle them. Give the consultant the play and the underlying logic:

**"Is it worth the investment?"**
Do the math together, with real numbers. If fees would consume most of the grant value, it's NOT worth it — say so and rightsize down. If the math works, show it concretely as a multiple of the fee. Mention that consultation fees credit toward subscriptions.

**"There probably aren't many grants for us"**
Reframe: nobody can make that statement — grants change constantly, new programs launch every fiscal year. Give one concrete hidden-opportunity example relevant to their situation. Anchor to the platform's track record of surfacing grants clients missed on their own. Position as risk mitigation: even one program a year carries the ROI.

**"We already know how to apply / we've done this before"**
Acknowledge competence — don't patronize. Pivot to value beyond application help: rules change every year; what worked last year may not work now. Share insider knowledge they wouldn't have. Position ongoing intelligence, not application writing, as the value.

**"How much of our time will this take?"**
Be honest — it scales with complexity. Hiring grants: barely any client input. Export grants: 2-3 hours total over a couple weeks (their market knowledge is needed). Complex innovation grants: more involved, needs an internal point person. Bandwidth constraints are themselves evidence for managed service when appropriate.

**"We don't have bandwidth right now"**
Validate it. It's evidence for managed service: "That's exactly why we exist — we do it, you provide the input." If they genuinely can't engage at all, don't force it; plant a seed with a specific trigger: "When you start hiring for that new location, call us."

**"We applied before and got rejected for a weird reason"**
Don't dismiss — investigate. Leverage insider contacts and program relationships to understand what happened. Rejection is intelligence for a stronger re-application.

**"How do you differ from [competitor]?"**
See Competitive Differentiation above. Core: 30+ years of grant expertise vs. tech-only platforms that match but can't advise. "We're not just matching — we're interpreting. We call the grantors; we understand what they're actually looking for."

**"I want guarantees"**
Be direct: there are none. Context: the success-rate stat comes from managed service; portal self-serve depends on the user's effort. Reframe what the portal delivers: best practices from managed-service experience, hours saved weekly on research, notifications they'd never find alone.

**"We already applied on our own and it worked"**
Different from "we've done this before" — this person is actively succeeding without help. Don't sell what they don't need. The FOMO angle is honest here: "The risk isn't the applications you're doing — it's the ones you don't know about." If they genuinely don't need managed service, Starter is the honest recommendation.

**"Can you just tell me three grants I should apply for?"**
That's asking for Pro-level strategy on a free call. Hold the line, kindly: that level of specificity needs a dedicated strategist who understands the full situation — that's what the paid tiers are. The matching platform is the self-serve answer. Don't make them feel rejected — redirect to the right tier. (Note this differs from internal use: consultants themselves always get specific program names — see the skill overview's boundaries.)

**"I'm worried we're too niche"**
Validate — some businesses genuinely fall between categories (recreational facilities, trading companies, creative businesses). Be honest about what fits and what doesn't: "I'd love to have you as a client. I just don't know that I can add the value you need in [niche area]. But here's what IS a fit..." Capture what works (student hiring and training are nearly universal). If they need municipal, indigenous community, or sports grants — outside Granted's core — say so directly.

## How to Use This

When a consultant asks for conversation prep:
1. Identify the prospect's sophistication level and stakeholder structure from what's known
2. Pull the 2-3 objections most likely for this profile and the relevant plays
3. Refresh any stats the consultant might quote (`get_program_stats`, `search_recent_wins`)
4. Output a brief: likely dynamics, recommended framing, the plays with their reasoning, and any analogies suited to this client's world
